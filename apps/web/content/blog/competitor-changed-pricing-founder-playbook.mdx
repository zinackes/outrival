A competitor changed their pricing. You found out from a tweet, a customer, or — if you're lucky — a brief that scoped it for you before your coffee. Either way, there's now a small adrenaline spike and a strong urge to do something about it by the next standup.

Resist that urge for a day. A pricing change is a signal, not an order, and the most expensive mistakes in pricing are reactive ones. Here's the playbook I'd run — the same sequence we bake into Outrival's briefs — before you change a single number of your own.

## Step 1 — Verify it's real, and scope it exactly

Before anything else, pin down *what actually changed*. Screenshots travel faster than facts, and "Competitor X raised prices" is almost always less true or less relevant than it first sounds. Answer these:

- **Is it live for everyone, or a test?** Pricing pages get A/B tested and geo-split constantly. What one person screenshotted may not be what your buyers see. Check from a couple of contexts before you believe it's global.
- **Which plans moved?** A change to the top enterprise tier means something completely different from a change to the entry plan you actually compete with. Map the change plan by plan.
- **Price, or packaging?** Often the headline number didn't move — a feature moved between tiers, a plan was renamed, an add-on was introduced, or a free tier quietly disappeared. Repackaging can matter more than a price delta, and it's easy to miss.
- **Are existing customers grandfathered?** A raise that only applies to new logos is a different competitive fact than one that hits their install base. It tells you how confident they are and how much churn risk they're willing to take.

You want to walk out of this step able to say, in one sentence, exactly what changed and for whom. "They removed the free tier and their new entry plan is $39, up from $29, for new customers only" is a fact you can act on. "They raised prices" is a rumor.

## Step 2 — Read what the move is signaling

Pricing is strategy made visible. Once you've scoped the change, classify it — because the *type* of move tells you more than the number:

- **Moving up-market** (raising entry prices, adding an enterprise tier): they're chasing bigger deals and, often, leaving the low end more exposed. That may be a door opening for you.
- **Moving down-market** (adding a cheaper plan, a free tier): they're going for volume and top-of-funnel. Expect more head-to-head deals with price-sensitive buyers.
- **Simplifying** (fewer plans, clearer packaging): usually a maturity move, and a sign they were losing deals to confusion. Watch whether your own pricing now looks *more* complicated by comparison.
- **Raising across the board:** either confidence (they have pricing power) or margin pressure (they need revenue). The grandfathering answer from Step 1 usually tells you which.

You're not just noting that a price changed. You're inferring where they're pointing the company. That inference is the actual intelligence.

## Step 3 — Assess the impact by segment

Here's where most reactions go wrong: they treat "a competitor got more expensive" as uniformly good and "cheaper" as uniformly bad. It's never uniform. Break it down by *your* segments:

- **Where do you actually overlap?** If the change hit a plan or a buyer you don't compete for, the correct response may be nothing at all.
- **Did it change your relative position?** If their entry plan is now above yours, you just became the value option for price-sensitive buyers — a wedge worth pressing. If they undercut you, a specific segment of your funnel just got harder.
- **Does it open or close a wedge?** A removed free tier strands their most price-sensitive users. A new cheap plan invites a comparison you may want to shape before they do.

The output of this step is a short list: *these* segments are affected, in *this* direction, by *this* much. Everything else, you consciously set aside. A change that doesn't touch a segment you care about is a change you note and move on from — writing that down is a decision, not a dodge.

## Step 4 — Decide whether to respond (usually: don't)

This is the step with the most gravity and the least glamour. The default should be **do not change your price**. Reactive repricing is how you end up in a margin race you didn't choose, anchored to a competitor's strategy instead of your own. A competitor's move is information about *them*; it's not automatically a mandate for *you*.

Run through the ladder of responses, cheapest first:

1. **No change.** If your positioning holds and the overlap is small, the disciplined move is to do nothing to your pricing and simply make sure sales knows the new landscape.
2. **A positioning response.** Change the *story*, not the number. If they went up-market, lean into being the accessible, transparent option. If they got complicated, lean into being simple. This is where most good responses live.
3. **A packaging response.** Adjust what's in your tiers or add a plan to meet a wedge — slower than a story change, faster and safer than a price change.
4. **A price response.** The nuclear option. Only if the change genuinely resets your value in a segment that matters, and only after you've modeled what it does to your margin and your existing customers.

If you find yourself reaching for option 4 in the first 48 hours, that's usually the adrenaline talking, not the analysis.

## Step 5 — Communicate the right thing to the right people

Whatever you decide, close the loop internally before you think about anything customer-facing:

- **Arm sales immediately.** They'll hit this in live deals before you've finished deliberating. Give them one clean line — what changed and how to frame it — and update the relevant battlecard. This is the highest-leverage output of the whole exercise.
- **Brief the exec team** with the one-sentence scope and your recommendation, so nobody hears it secondhand and panics.
- **Go customer-facing only if you must.** Most competitor pricing changes need zero public response from you. If yours genuinely does, make it about your value, never a defensive reaction to theirs.

## The 48-hour checklist

When a competitor pricing change lands, in order:

1. **Scope it** — which plans, price vs packaging, test vs global, grandfathered or not.
2. **Classify it** — up-market, down-market, simplification, or across-the-board.
3. **Segment the impact** — who's affected, which direction, how much.
4. **Choose a response tier** — nothing, positioning, packaging, or (rarely) price.
5. **Communicate** — arm sales, brief execs, stay quiet publicly unless there's a real reason not to.

The thread through all five steps: **move slowly on purpose.** The competitor already made their decision; your advantage is that you don't have to make yours in a hurry.
