If you've tried to find out what [Crayon](/vs/crayon) costs, you already know the answer: you can't, at least not from Crayon. There's no pricing page, no rate card, no self-serve tier. Every deal is custom and sales-led, disclosed only after a discovery call. So this post does the next best thing — it pulls together what independent sources actually report, dates every figure, and flags what's an estimate versus a fact.

One caveat up front, and it's the important one: **Crayon does not publish pricing, so every dollar amount below is a third-party estimate or an aggregate of reported deals, not an official number.** Treat any single figure as directional. The only price you can fully trust is the one on a quote with your name on it.

## The official answer: there isn't one

Crayon sells three tiers — **Essentials, Professional, and Enterprise** — and routes everyone through a [pricing-inquiry form](https://www.crayon.co/pricing-inquiry) rather than a checkout. On [G2](https://www.g2.com/products/crayon-crayon/pricing), the pricing tab reads, in effect, "Crayon has not provided pricing information for this product." That's not an oversight; it's the model. Pricing scales with the number of competitors you track, the number of seats, the feature tier, and the contract length, and it's all negotiated.

That model has a real consequence for a buyer: you can't budget before you talk to sales, and you can't compare tiers without a scoping call. For a large team that's routine. For a founder or a solo product marketer trying to size a line item, it's friction before you've seen a single feature.

## What the procurement data says

The most concrete numbers come from procurement platforms that aggregate real closed deals. As of **February 2026**, [Vendr's marketplace](https://www.vendr.com/marketplace/crayon) reports a **median annual contract value of about $29,500**, drawn from 92 recorded purchases, with a range spanning roughly **$12,600 at the low end to $46,500 at the high end** and an average discount around 20% off the initial quote.

Layer the review-site and analyst estimates on top and a consistent band emerges:

- **Entry / Essentials:** roughly **$15,000–$25,000 per year** for a small team tracking a handful of competitors with a few seats.
- **Professional:** mid-tier, scaling with competitors and seats.
- **Enterprise:** **$100,000+** for large deployments — many competitors, many seats, custom integrations.

Most third-party summaries put the "typical" Crayon contract in the **$20,000–$40,000 per year** range, with starting points cited anywhere from ~$15,000 to ~$25,000 depending on the source. That spread is itself the point: without public pricing, the honest answer is a band, not a number.

## The number that isn't on the invoice

Here's the part that matters more than the license, and it's the part buyers most often miss. Crayon is a platform that **assumes a dedicated owner** — a competitive-intelligence analyst or a product marketer whose job is to curate the feeds, write the battlecards, and push insights to the rest of the company. Several buyer breakdowns note that this labor is typically the **single largest line item** in the real total cost of ownership, larger than the software itself.

Do the arithmetic and it gets sobering fast. A [cost-of-ownership analysis](https://www.userintuition.ai/posts/competitive-intelligence-cost/) estimates that a nominally $40,000 Crayon subscription actually costs somewhere around **$66,000–$123,000 a year** once you include the person required to make it useful. If that owner doesn't exist, the reported failure mode is predictable and common: the dashboards go unopened and the deployment drifts toward shelfware. You paid for a platform and staffed it with nobody.

This is the number to bring to a budget conversation. The license is the down payment; the analyst is the mortgage.

## What actually drives your quote

If you do go to a scoping call, these are the levers that move the price:

- **Competitors tracked.** Pricing scales heavily here — monitoring 5–10 competitors costs meaningfully less than 25–50.
- **Seats.** More users, higher tier, higher price.
- **Feature tier.** Essentials vs Professional vs Enterprise gates capabilities like advanced battlecards and integrations.
- **Add-ons and services.** Battlecards, custom integrations, dedicated customer success, and professional services can add **15–30%** on top of the base platform.
- **Contract length.** Multi-year commitments lower the effective annual rate — at the cost of flexibility to renegotiate or switch.
- **Escalation clauses.** Multi-year deals frequently carry annual price increases in the **3–7%** range unless you negotiate a cap.

A practical negotiation note buyers repeat: cap annual increases explicitly (say, to CPI or a flat 3%), push for a Q4 or annual-prepay discount, and don't accept a multi-year term unless the effective rate genuinely beats an annual one.

## How that compares in the category

Crayon doesn't set its prices in a vacuum, and the numbers make more sense next to its nearest peers. [Klue](/vs/klue), the other name that comes up in almost every enterprise CI evaluation, sits in a similar entry band — third-party reports put it around **$15,000–$20,000 a year** to start, often landing slightly higher than Crayon at comparable scope. Kompyte, aimed a little lower in the market, is consistently reported as the cheaper of the three. In other words, the entire "legacy enterprise CI" category clusters in the same five-figure, sales-led, annual-contract shape. If Crayon's model frustrates you, switching to Klue mostly buys you a different logo on the same kind of contract.

That's worth internalizing before you spend three discovery calls comparing them. The meaningful fork isn't Crayon versus Klue versus Kompyte — they're variations on one buying motion. The meaningful fork is *that motion* (analyst-run, five-figure, annually committed) versus a self-serve, publicly priced tool you can turn on this afternoon. Which side of that fork you belong on has almost nothing to do with the price delta between the incumbents and almost everything to do with whether you have the headcount to run a platform like this at all.

## So is it worth it?

That depends entirely on who's asking, and I'll be straight about it rather than pretend everyone should switch.

If you're a **large organization with a real CI function** — an analyst who lives in the tool, a sales team consuming battlecards daily, and the budget to match — Crayon is a mature, capable platform and the five-figure entry is defensible. It was built for exactly that motion.

If you're a **founder, a solo PMM, or a small team** without a dedicated CI owner, the math is heavy in the other direction. You're being asked to commit five figures to a platform that assumes headcount you don't have, before you've seen a price.
